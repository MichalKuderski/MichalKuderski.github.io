CASE 01

Medicare Provider Spending Analysis

CMS Medicare Part B Claims · Illinois Psychiatry · 2023

Tools: PostgreSQL · SQL · Excel · CMS Medicare Part B data
Methods: CTEs · window functions · reimbursement analysis · KPI dashboarding · outlier detection

Who actually gets to see a psychiatrist in Illinois?

This project analyzes 2,460 CMS Medicare psychiatry claim records across 692 Illinois providers and 96 procedure codes to evaluate healthcare spending, utilization, reimbursement patterns, and provider-level cost variation.

I built a normalized PostgreSQL database, wrote analytical SQL queries using CTEs, subqueries, aggregations, and window functions, and developed an Excel dashboard to summarize key performance indicators such as Medicare payment per service, cost per beneficiary, procedure-level spending, and charge-to-payment differences.

The analysis surfaced two important decision points: CPT 99214 was the largest cost driver at $5.6M, representing 20.8% of Medicare payments, and telephone-based service delivery varied sharply by city, with Elk Grove Village at 76.7% compared with Chicago at 1.9%. These findings suggest that healthcare leaders should evaluate not only provider spending, but also access barriers, care delivery mode, and whether transportation or telehealth infrastructure may be limiting patient access.

Question

Where are psychiatry costs and access patterns concentrated?

Finding

CPT 99214 drove 20.8% of Medicare payments, and telephone-service share varied sharply by city — from 76.7% in Elk Grove Village to 1.9% in Chicago.

Recommendation

Evaluate telehealth access, transportation support, and provider distribution before assuming staffing is the only constraint.

The question

A community mental health organization has a fixed budget and a service area spanning dense city blocks and spread-out suburbs. Where should it put clinicians — and where is the constraint something other than headcount?

The data

CMS publishes every Medicare Part B claim line by provider and procedure code. I filtered the 2023 file to Illinois psychiatry — 2,460 claim records across 692 providers — and loaded it into a normalized PostgreSQL schema: providers, procedure codes, and a utilization fact table.

The method

Six analytical queries using CTEs, window functions, and subqueries. Five answered the expected cost questions. The sixth asked something the brief didn't: how are these services actually being delivered?

Why it matters

A 40-point gap between suburb and city isn't clinical preference. It reads as a transportation and access constraint — patients who can't easily reach a provider get care by phone instead. That changes the intervention: the suburbs may need transport support or telehealth infrastructure before they need another clinician. Different budget line, different plan.

The trap I avoided

Submitted charges are not a cost measure. Providers routinely bill 2–5× the allowed amount, so any analysis anchored on charges overstates cost badly. I anchored on actual payment throughout.

What I'd do next

Join against provider density and transit coverage by ZIP to separate "no provider nearby" from "provider nearby but unreachable." The current data can't distinguish them — and the fix for each is different.

Limitations

  • Claims data shows utilization and payment patterns — not clinical outcomes, care quality, or patient satisfaction.
  • It reflects what was billed, not appointment availability or how long a patient waited to be seen.
  • Telephone-service share is a proxy for access, not a direct measurement of transportation barriers or provider capacity — it shows how care was delivered, not why.
  • Several of the highest telephone-share cities — Elk Grove Village and DeKalb among them — have only one billing provider. A single provider's practice style can dominate a city's percentage, so city-level findings here are leads worth verifying against a larger sample, not standalone conclusions.

BIO

About

I'm a recent graduate with a B.S. in Statistics from Northern Illinois University, and most of what I enjoy about this work happens in the gap between a number being correct and a number being useful.

The Medicare project is a good example. Five of my six queries answered the cost questions you'd expect. The sixth — the one about how services were actually delivered — is the only one that would have changed a decision. I've gotten more interested in finding that sixth query.

I minored in geography alongside mathematics, which turned out to be less of a detour than it sounds. The finding I'm proudest of is a geographic one: the same service, delivered completely differently depending on whether a patient could physically get to a provider. Distance is a variable, and it's usually missing from the table.

Healthcare is where I've gone deepest, because the data is messy in ways that matter. A submitted charge isn't a cost. A missing provider isn't the same as an unreachable one. Getting those distinctions right is most of the job.

But the shape of the problem isn't unique to healthcare. It's the same in supply chain and logistics: a system moving finite resources through constrained paths, generating data about it, and not always noticing what that data is saying. I'm interested in analyst work anywhere that pattern shows up — healthcare operations, supply chain management, logistics and logistics technology, or any industry that needs someone to look carefully at the numbers and say what they actually mean.
